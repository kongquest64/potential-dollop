# Why Hasn't Nintendo Died? Here's What Actually Keeps It Alive

*Narration script (~11 min). On-screen visual noted under each beat.*

> **[hero]** −97%

In 1983, the American video game industry collapsed. In just two years, home video game revenue fell by roughly ninety-seven percent. Retailers swore off games entirely. Atari, the company that had defined the medium, hauled truckloads of unsold cartridges to a landfill in the New Mexico desert and buried them.

> **[graveyard]** 

Into that wreckage walked a century-old Japanese playing card company. Four decades later, look at who else was making game hardware back then. Atari. Coleco. Mattel. Magnavox. NEC. SNK. Panasonic. Sega. Every one of them is out of the console business. Nintendo is still here. And it isn't just surviving. Its last console sold more than a hundred and fifty million units, and its new one is the fastest-selling machine it has ever made.

> **[title]** Why Hasn't Nintendo Died?

So here's a question that sounds strange, given all that success. Why hasn't Nintendo died? Because it really should have. Several times.

> **[timeline]** Five near-death experiences

The Virtual Boy, a red-and-black headache machine that sold well under a million units. The Nintendo 64, which bet on cartridges and lost Final Fantasy to Sony. The GameCube, which finished a distant third. The 3DS, whose launch went so badly its price was slashed within six months. And the Wii U, which sold just thirteen and a half million units, as Nintendo posted its first annual losses in decades. For a lot of companies, any one of those would have been fatal.

> **[list]** What actually decides it

The usual answer is: Mario. Great characters, great games. That's true, but it isn't enough. Sega had Sonic. Atari had the most famous brand in the world. Great games explain why Nintendo succeeds. They don't explain why it survives its failures. For that, you have to look at how the company is built. And when you do, four things stand out.

## Part 01 — A Toy Company's Instincts

> **[chapter]** A Toy Company's Instincts

Part one. Nintendo is a toy company.

> **[timeline]** A company in search of a second act

Nintendo was founded in Kyoto in 1889 by Fusajiro Yamauchi, to make hanafuda, hand-painted playing cards. For much of the twentieth century, it was a company searching for a second act. Under Fusajiro's great-grandson, Hiroshi Yamauchi, it tried a taxi service, instant rice, and, reportedly, love hotels. Almost none of it worked.

> **[hero]** 1,000,000+

What did work was toys. In 1966, a young maintenance engineer named Gunpei Yokoi built an extending grabber arm to amuse himself. Yamauchi saw it, and told him to turn it into a product. The Ultra Hand sold over a million units. Yokoi was moved into product development, and Nintendo quietly became a toy company.

> **[statement]** Nobody buys a yo-yo because of its processor.

That matters more than it sounds. A toy company doesn't sell specifications. Nobody buys a yo-yo because of its processor. A toy has to be understood in seconds, and be fun in your hands. That instinct, fun first, explained by touch, runs straight through the NES, the Game Boy, the Wii Remote, and the Joy-Con.

> **[hero]** 61.9M

When Nintendo brought the NES to America after the crash, retailers wanted nothing to do with video games. So Nintendo didn't call it one. It was the Nintendo Entertainment System, and it came with a little plastic robot called R.O.B., so that wary stores would shelve it as a toy. It worked. The NES sold over sixty million units and revived the entire industry.

## Part 02 — Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology

> **[chapter]** Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology

Part two. Lateral thinking with withered technology.

> **[define]** 

Yokoi eventually gave his philosophy a name. Lateral thinking with withered technology. Withered means mature: cheap, reliable, well understood. Lateral means using it in a way nobody expected. The Game and Watch came from Yokoi watching a bored businessman on a bullet train, fiddling with a calculator. Cheap screens, calculator chips, a simple game. It sold over forty million units.

> **[versus]** 

The Game Boy, in 1989, was the purest example. Its screen was a murky, greenish monochrome. Its rivals, the Atari Lynx and later the Sega Game Gear, had backlit color screens. They were more advanced in every way you could measure. They also ate through six double-A batteries in a few hours. The Game Boy ran for well over ten hours on four, cost less, and came with Tetris. With the Game Boy Color, it sold nearly a hundred and nineteen million units. The Lynx and the Game Gear are footnotes.

> **[bars]** Lateral thinking, in units sold (millions)

The pattern keeps repeating. In the mid-2000s, Sony and Microsoft were racing toward high definition. Nintendo's Wii was barely more powerful than the GameCube. What it had was a motion controller your grandmother could understand in ten seconds. It sold more than a hundred million units, and turned up in retirement homes. The DS did the same for handhelds. Two screens, a stylus, and games like Brain Age that sold to people who had never called themselves gamers. A hundred and fifty-four million units.

> **[versus]** 

The strategic logic is simple. If you compete on power, you're in a spending war with companies far bigger than you, and the winner is whoever can afford to lose money the longest. If you compete on a new way to play, there's nobody to outspend you, because nobody else is in that market.

## Part 03 — Games Are the Product

> **[chapter]** Games Are the Product

Part three. The games are the product.

> **[timeline]** Shigeru Miyamoto

At its core, Nintendo is a software company that happens to build the machines its software needs. Shigeru Miyamoto joined as a staff artist in 1977. One of his first big assignments was to rescue a warehouse of unsold arcade cabinets. The result was Donkey Kong, and its hero, a carpenter called Jumpman, would become Mario. Miyamoto's designs famously start from a feeling. His childhood memories of exploring the hills and caves around Kyoto became The Legend of Zelda.

> **[quote]** A delayed game is eventually good, but a rushed game is forever bad.

There's a line often attributed to him. A delayed game is eventually good, but a rushed game is forever bad. Whether or not he put it exactly that way, Nintendo behaves as if it believes it. Games slip, sometimes for years, until they feel right. Breath of the Wild was delayed repeatedly. So was its sequel. Both were hailed as instant classics.

> **[statement]** Competitors build a box and invite the world to fill it.

And because Nintendo makes the hardware, it designs the two together. The Nintendo 64's analog stick was developed alongside Super Mario 64. The Wii Remote and Wii Sports grew up together. Competitors build a powerful box and invite the world to fill it. Nintendo builds a toy, and the game that explains it.

> **[list]** Characters that never expire

Over decades, that produced the most valuable stable of characters in the industry. Mario. Zelda. Pokémon. Kirby. Animal Crossing. Smash Bros. Pokémon, which Nintendo co-owns, is the highest-grossing media franchise in history. Mario Kart 8 Deluxe has sold more than sixty million copies on a single console. And these games don't expire. Nintendo rarely discounts its own titles deeply, because a Mario game from six years ago still sells like a new release.

> **[statement]** Consoles come and go. The characters walk onto the next machine.

This is the part that makes failure survivable. When a Nintendo console flops, the people who bought it still got Mario Kart, Zelda, and Smash. And those franchises walk straight onto the next machine, with their fans right behind them.

## Part 04 — A Fortress Balance Sheet

> **[chapter]** A Fortress Balance Sheet

Part four. The fortress.

> **[list]** Built for bad weather

Which brings us to the least glamorous reason Nintendo survives, and maybe the most important. Money. Hiroshi Yamauchi ran Nintendo like a company that expected bad weather. Unlike its rivals, Nintendo has generally insisted that its hardware sell at a profit, or close to it, from early on. Sony and Microsoft can subsidize a console because they have other huge businesses. Nintendo doesn't. So every machine has to pay its own way. It also keeps an enormous pile of cash and essentially no debt. In good years, critics called that capital lazy. Then the Wii U happened, and the cash did exactly what it was there for. It bought time.

> **[bars]** The second leg to stand on (units sold, millions)

Then there's the handheld safety net. For nearly three decades, Nintendo ran two separate hardware lines. When the home console stumbled, the handheld carried the company. The GameCube struggled, but the Game Boy Advance sold over eighty million units. The Wii U flopped, but the 3DS sold more than seventy-five million. There was always a second leg to stand on.

> **[quote]** Employees who fear that they may be laid off will not be able to develop software titles that impress people around the world.

Leadership matters here too. In 2011, after the 3DS price cut, president Satoru Iwata cut his own salary in half. When investors later pushed for layoffs, he refused. His argument was that employees who fear being laid off will not make software that impresses people around the world. That kind of decision is only possible if the balance sheet gives you room to make it.

## Part 05 — All Four at Once

> **[chapter]** All Four at Once

Now put all four together, and look at the Switch.

> **[list]** The Switch, 2017

By 2017, Nintendo was coming off the Wii U, its worst-selling home console. Smartphones were eating the casual market the DS once owned. Plenty of analysts said it was time to quit hardware and put Mario on the iPhone, the way Sega had gone software-only. Instead, Nintendo merged its two hardware lines into one. The Switch ran on an off-the-shelf mobile chip. Withered technology, again. It was a toy you understood in a second. Pick it up, take it with you, hand a Joy-Con to a friend. And it launched alongside Breath of the Wild, a game that had been allowed to take years.

> **[bars]** Nintendo hardware, lifetime units (millions)

The Switch went on to sell more than a hundred and fifty million units, putting it in the same tier as the DS and the PlayStation 2 among the best-selling consoles ever. Its successor, the Switch 2, launched in June 2025 and sold three and a half million units in four days, the fastest start of any Nintendo hardware. The company that was told to give up on hardware built one of the most successful hardware businesses in history.

> **[hero]** $1.3B+

Nintendo has also learned to take its characters beyond games without giving them away. Theme parks with Universal. The Super Mario Bros. Movie in 2023, which grossed over a billion dollars worldwide. Each of those is, in the end, an advertisement for the games.

> **[list]** Not invincible

So is Nintendo invincible? No. Its model has real weaknesses. Every six or seven years, it bets the company on a brand new idea, and some of those ideas will be wrong. The same conservatism that protects it also makes it slow. Its online services have lagged behind for years. And its hard line on prices, emulation, and fan projects costs it real goodwill.

> **[versus]** 

But compare the failure modes. Atari collapsed because it flooded the market with low-quality games and lost the trust of players. Sega burned through its cash and its customers' patience with too many machines in too few years, and couldn't absorb the Dreamcast. Most of the companies on that list died because one bad bet consumed everything.

> **[list]** What actually keeps it alive

Nintendo is built so that a bad bet can't consume everything. Hardware that pays for itself. Cash that buys time. Characters that outlive any console. And a toy maker's instinct to find a new way to play, instead of fighting someone bigger on their terms.

> **[statement]** Nintendo doesn't avoid failure.

That's the real answer. It isn't that Nintendo doesn't fail. It fails all the time. Loudly, publicly, and expensively. It's that the company is designed, very deliberately, to survive its failures long enough to reach the next idea. And every so often, the next idea is the Wii. Or the DS. Or the Switch.

> **[title]** 1889 → today

A hundred and thirty-seven years after it started selling playing cards, Nintendo is still in the business of making something fun to hold in your hands. Everything else is just how it pays for the next one.
